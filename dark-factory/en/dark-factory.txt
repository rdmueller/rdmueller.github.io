## Key claims

1. **Whether a dark factory fits depends on the risk class of the software, not
   on how much you believe in AI.** That turns a question of faith into an
   engineering decision.
2. **Every risk has a mitigation.** Each mitigation moves the line a little
   further toward autonomy.
3. **Code review doesn't scale anymore.** The bottleneck is volume: in five
   minutes, the AI writes code that takes five hours to review.
4. **Both camps are right, each for its own risk class.** The skeptics are right
   about the pacemaker, the hype crowd about the landing page. It only goes wrong
   when one side applies its rule to everything. Ban AI across the board and you
   give up the landing page. Vibe-code everything blindly and you forget that the
   team is still accountable.
5. **Human on the loop instead of human in the loop.** The human no longer checks
   every line. Instead, they define the environment the AI works in.

## Terms

- **Dark factory** has two sides. *Hands off:* the AI writes, tests and ships
  without a human stepping in. *Lights out:* nobody looks at the code anymore.
- Autonomy is **a scale, not a switch**: from handcrafted to dark factory.
- Ralf runs his side projects as a dark factory because they sit in the lower
  tiers. He explicitly doesn't recommend that for every kind of software.

## The product owner as a role model

Product owners never look at the source code. They hand requirements and
stories to the team, get software back and test it. Developers have to grow
into that role. That raises two questions: How can we trust the code? And does
this work for every kind of software?

## Risk classes: the Vibe-Coding Risk Radar

The Risk Radar rates a specific use case along five dimensions:

1. Code type
2. Language safety
3. Deployment context
4. Data sensitivity
5. Blast radius

The dimensions follow MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
**The highest rating in any single dimension determines the tier.**

Examples from the talk:

- **Static landing page:** Tier 1. Even a mistake like an emergency number in
  white text on a white background can be caught by a tool (a contrast check
  costs next to nothing).
- **Firmware for a medical device:** Tier 4.
- There are more classes in between: an internal dashboard is harmless, a
  payment service more critical.

For larger, modular software, you don't rate the whole system. You rate each
module. A frontend module can land in a different class than the backend.

The radar makes no claim to authority. It was vibe-coded itself, it's open
source on GitHub, and it's meant as a first proposal to help you get your
bearings. Disagreement and improvements are welcome. The ratings reflect today;
the models are getting better fast.

## Mitigations: the Harness Coverage Wheel

The LLM is like a noisy channel (Shannon; the analogy is Ingo Eichhorst's):
a specification goes in, software comes out, non-deterministic and noisy.
A damaged QR code stays readable because it carries error-correction bits. So
the question is: what error correction do I add to the channel?

- The simplest error correction is the **compiler**. Whatever compiles has no
  syntax errors a human would need to review. With interpreted languages, you
  need tests to get the same guarantee.
- Every check feeds errors back to the LLM, and the LLM corrects itself.
- The question shifts: Which aspects does the tooling cover, and which are left
  for human review?

The **Harness Coverage Wheel** collects these aspects: **69 layers of error
correction in nine sections**, from the compiler to chaos engineering. The shape
of each dot tells you how to get a layer in place:

- **Circle:** an external tool with a fixed standard (e.g. a language's syntax).
  Switch it on in the build pipeline and you're done.
- **Triangle:** the project defines the standard itself (e.g. domain tests).
  It takes human effort up front, then runs on its own.
- **Square:** a mix of both (not covered in the talk, but drawn on the wheel
  page).
- **Person icon:** twelve layers that only a human can cover.

Two examples of risks and their mitigations:

| Risk | Mitigations |
|---|---|
| Code ships fast but unchecked | automated tests, review gate, property-based tests |
| Insecure dependencies, hallucinated packages | dependency and container scans, SBOM, pinned versions |

The review itself can be a layer too: an AI review with the right model finds
more today than a human does.

**The risk class sets the dial.** For a prototype, the compiler is almost
enough. For safety-critical software, all 69 layers count. The map stays the
same; only what's mandatory changes.

## From radar to governance: two phases, six steps

**Phase 1: Assess**

1. The team breaks the software down into modules.
2. It rates each module along the radar's five dimensions and assigns it a
   tier. It may deviate from the recommendation if it has good reasons.
3. The rating goes into an Architecture Decision Record (ADR). The lead
   developer or the security team reviews and approves it.

**Phase 2: Mitigate**

4. The team picks mitigations that fit the tier and the technology. Each company
   decides for itself which layer is mandatory in which tier.
5. The AI implements the mitigations, that is, the pipeline and the gates.
6. A second ADR records how the risk is mitigated. Again, a human reviews and
   approves it.

The AI helps with almost every step: breaking the system down, rating modules,
writing ADRs, building pipelines. What used to need a toolsmith is now cheap to
set up. In the end, one question remains: Which aspects does a human still have
to check personally?

For transparency, you can tell the AI "Look at the wheel, look at this software,
and mark what's already covered." That gives you a dashboard with the risk and
the mitigations side by side.

## From the Q&A

- **Should a different model review the code than the one that wrote it?**
  Ralf doesn't think so; at their core, the training data is the same. In his
  experience, the same model with a fresh context already gets you a long way. Only
  evaluations could settle the question. What's certain: a review by the same
  model with a fresh context beats no AI review at all.
- **Without a good specification, doesn't the AI just produce nonsense?** Yes,
  and you can use that: build three architectures in an hour and run a load test
  against them. That wasn't possible before.
- **What if the big models get worse or go away?** That's when the wheel pays
  off. Linters, compilers and domain tests check a small local model just as
  strictly. It takes more loops and tokens, but the checks still work. Every
  improvement to the harness makes the model easier to swap out.

## Conclusion

Mitigations move the line. The risk class isn't a verdict; it's the starting
point. The radar and the wheel shine a light into the dark factory: you can
see the risk and the mitigations, and you know why you trust a module.

Dark factory for the landing page. Handcrafted, with a safety net, for the
pacemaker. Both are right.
