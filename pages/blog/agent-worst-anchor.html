"Agent" Is the Worst Semantic Anchor in AI

🤖 "Agent" — the worst Semantic Anchor in AI right now.

Ask a developer: "What's an Agent?" → An LLM with tool access that can autonomously complete tasks.

Ask a manager: "What's an Agent?" → That magic thing that solves all my problems and replaces my employees. 😄

Ask a customer service lead: "What's an Agent?" → The person picking up the phone in our call center.

Same word. Three completely different mental models. And everyone in the room assumes they're talking about the same thing.

This is exactly why "Agent" fails as a Semantic Anchor.

Semantic Anchors work because they tap into well-defined concepts from training data — terms so precisely established that an LLM immediately retrieves the correct definition. Think "TDD, London School" or "ADRs according to Nygard". One phrase, one meaning, zero ambiguity.

"Agent" is the opposite: a term hijacked by hype, stretched across disciplines, and carrying different baggage depending on who's in the room.

Compare it to "DRY" — short for "Don't Repeat Yourself". Yes, it also means "not wet" in English. But two signals make it a strong anchor: the capitalization (DRY vs. dry) and the software development context. The LLM resolves this correctly every time.

"Agent" has no such saving grace. The context doesn't help, because every domain claims it.

So what's the fix?

Define it explicitly in your team's context — for example in an `agents.md` file that every AI session loads:

> "When we say Agent, we mean an LLM-based system with access to tools that can autonomously complete multi-step tasks — not a human, not a magic wand."

That's not a workaround. That's the whole point of project-specific Semantic Anchors: closing the communication gap that's otherwise filled by intuition and assumption.

The word "Agent" isn't broken. It just needs a definition.
