Agentic Foundry: The Mould Decides

I've been calling my setup a dark factory. Last month I even gave a talk with that word in the title. The longer I used it, the more one half of it bothered me. The "dark" is right. The "factory" is wrong.

What "dark factory" gets right

The term comes from lights-out manufacturing. A lights-out plant runs without people on the floor, so nobody needs to switch the lights on. Robot makers have run plants like this for years. Applied to software, the term describes a degree of automation, and for my side projects it is accurate. No human reviews the code. The agents write it and ship it. I look at the running result in the browser, not at the code in an editor.

What it gets wrong

A factory makes the same thing a thousand times. That is the whole point of a factory: you pay once for the design and the tooling, and every further unit is cheap. Software has never worked like that. The second copy of a program is a file copy. The expensive part is the first one, and in my setup every single thing that comes out is a first one. A simulator for a drawing robot one week, a transcription pipeline the next. No two alike. Calling that a factory conjures up an assembly line that doesn't exist.

The words I tried and threw out

Manufactory, or Manufaktur in German, was my first candidate. It sounds like quality and small series. But the word comes from Latin manu factum, made by hand, and that is precisely what no longer happens here. The idea comes from a human. The making does not.

Atelier lives on the hand of the master. Someone with taste shapes every piece. In my setup there is a master, but he never touches the material.

Software factory is taken, and taken in the wrong sense. Japanese firms such as Hitachi and Toshiba ran "software factories" from the 1970s on, built around standardized processes and reuse; Michael Cusumano documented them in his 1991 book Japan's Software Factories. In 2004, Jack Greenfield and Keith Short at Microsoft published Software Factories, about assembling applications from patterns, models and frameworks. Both mean repeatable, industrialized production of similar systems. That is the opposite of a one-off.

Workshop would be a fine word in German, where Werkstatt means a place where things get made. In English it mostly means a seminar with sticky notes.

Foundry: three generations of the same idea

A foundry casts metal. The word comes from French fonderie and Latin fundere, to pour or to melt. It is not related to "to found" in the sense of founding a company, which comes from fundare, to lay a foundation. The two verbs only collided in English spelling.

The idea behind the word has been reused twice, and each time the core stayed the same.

In a metal foundry, somebody brings a pattern. The foundry makes a mould from it and pours. The pour is the process, and the mould decides what comes out. A bad mould gives a bad casting no matter how good the bronze.

A type foundry cast letters. A punchcutter carved the letterform once, and the foundry cast thousands of pieces of type from the resulting matrices. The term outlived the lead. Companies that design and sell digital fonts still call themselves type foundries, with no metal in the building.

A semiconductor foundry makes chips for other companies. Morris Chang founded TSMC in 1987 as the first pure-play foundry. TSMC doesn't design chips. Customers like Apple and Nvidia bring the design, and TSMC casts it into silicon.

In all three cases the form comes from outside, and the pouring is the process.

Für deutsche Leser: Gießerei, nicht Manufaktur

Im Deutschen heißt das Bild Gießerei, und der Unterschied zur Manufaktur ist der Punkt des ganzen Textes. Wer Manufaktur hört, denkt an Handarbeit und an den Meister, der jedes Stück formt. Wer Gießerei hört, denkt an Form und Guss. Die Form kommt von außen, der Guss ist der Prozess.

Die Brücke zur Schriftgießerei liegt hierzulande besonders nahe. Gutenberg hat seine Lettern nicht geschnitzt, er hat sie gegossen, mit dem Handgießinstrument aus einer Matrize. Seine Erfindung war weniger die Presse als der Guss: eine Form, aus der sich beliebig viele gleiche Buchstaben herstellen ließen. Die Schriftgießereien in Frankfurt und Offenbach haben dieses Handwerk bis ins 20. Jahrhundert getragen. Wer wissen will, was eine Agentic Foundry ist: eine Gießerei, in der die Spec die Matrize ist.

What this means for the work

Agentic coding without human code review is a foundry. I make the mould: the idea, written down as a spec with its constraints and decisions. The agents pour. When the casting is wrong, I don't file down the casting. I fix the mould and pour again.

My drawing robot from this summer shows the pattern. Claude designed every part as a parametric OpenSCAD model. The first version needed 24 screws. I didn't edit a single part. I said "too many screws", the model switched to crush ribs, and the next pour had nine. Before printing nine hours of parts, we printed a fit test with every critical bore in three sizes. I measured, reported "press fit" or "too loose", and the numbers went back into the model, not into the parts. The model was the mould, and all my work went into it.

That is the consequence of the word. In a factory, quality lives in the process on the floor, and an inspector at the end of the line catches what the process missed. In a foundry without an inspector, quality lives in the mould. So the work moves from the code to the spec, and the skill that matters is mould-making: knowing which constraints to carve in and which to leave to the pour. The spec can be a document, a set of architecture decisions, a parametric model, or a test suite. Whatever shapes the pour is the mould.

None of this says every project should run this way. Whether you can leave the inspector out depends on the risk class of the software, and that was the subject of the dark factory talk. Landing page, yes. Pacemaker firmware, no. The foundry picture doesn't change that answer. It changes what you do with your time once you have made it.

One more thing, since Foundry is also a product name at Palantir and at Microsoft. I'm not naming a tool. I'm describing a way of working, the way "dark factory" did.

If you run an agentic foundry, you're a mould-maker, not a factory worker.

What word do you use for your setup? And is the mould where your time goes?
